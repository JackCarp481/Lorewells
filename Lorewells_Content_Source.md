3. Scientific & Social Impact

Preserving Human Memory, Identity, and Wisdom for Future Generations

Lorewells is a professional oral-history preservation initiative dedicated to recording the life stories, memories, experiences, and wisdom of individuals for future generations.

The mission is simple: preserve human wisdom before it disappears.

This work is emotionally powerful, but it is also supported by research across psychology, sociology, gerontology, family studies, oral history, trauma recovery, and identity formation.

Research strongly suggests that family narratives can strengthen resilience in children and adolescents; intergenerational knowledge contributes to identity formation; storytelling can support well-being among older adults; life-review and reminiscence processes can increase meaning and life satisfaction; and oral histories preserve valuable cultural and historical information unavailable through traditional records.

Lorewells exists at the intersection of these findings. 

It is not merely a video project. It is a memory-preservation project, a family-strengthening project, a historical project, a legacy project, and a way to keep wisdom alive.
3.1 The Problem: Humanity Is Experiencing a Memory Crisis

Modern society possesses more information than any civilization in history, yet individuals are increasingly disconnected from their family histories, community histories, and inherited wisdom.

We have more data than ever, but less memory. 
We have more media than ever, but fewer preserved life stories. 
We have more communication tools than ever, but many families know less about their grandparents and great-grandparents than previous generations did.

Several forces contribute to this crisis: geographic mobility, declining multi-generational households, digital overload, and the loss of living witnesses.

Families now frequently live hundreds or thousands of miles apart. In earlier generations, storytelling often happened naturally around dinner tables, barns, porches, churches, shops, family farms, and shared work. 
Modern life has changed that. 
Families are scattered. Generations are separated. The natural transfer of stories has weakened.

Modern families may possess thousands of photos and videos, but very little meaningful context. A phone may contain 20,000 images, but almost no explanation of who the people are, what they endured, what they believed, what they learned, or why their lives mattered.

The last living witnesses of World War II are now very few. Korean War veterans are aging. Vietnam veterans are entering later stages of life. Cold War service members, old ranchers, retired teachers, longtime pastors, farmers, loggers, firefighters, law enforcement officers, and community builders carry firsthand memories that will soon be unrecoverable.
3.2 Why Family Stories Matter

One of the most relevant areas of research for Lorewells concerns the effect of family storytelling on children and adolescents.

Psychologists Marshall Duke and Robyn Fivush studied the role of family narratives in child development and resilience. Their work is associated with the 'Do You Know?' scale, which asks children questions such as where their grandparents grew up, how their parents met, what hardships their family endured, and what achievements or challenges shaped the family story.

Children who knew more about their family history tended to demonstrate stronger emotional resilience, higher self-esteem, better family functioning, and a stronger sense of control over their lives. The important point was not that the family stories were all positive. The strongest family narratives often included both hardship and recovery.

The most helpful stories are not simplistic stories of endless success. They say: our family has suffered before; our family has made mistakes before; our family has lost things before; our family has endured before; our family has recovered before.

This gives a child what researchers have called an intergenerational self: the understanding that 'I am part of a story that began before me and will continue after me.' Lorewells helps families preserve the raw material of intergenerational identity.

3.3 Narrative Identity and Human Psychology

Human beings do not experience life as isolated data points. We experience life as story.
Psychologist Dan P. McAdams and other researchers describe narrative identity as the internalized and evolving life story people form about who they are, where they came from, what they have endured, what they value, and where their life is going.

A person's identity is not built only from facts. It is built from meaning. Family stories play a major role in narrative identity. When someone knows the stories of previous generations, they gain a broader frame for understanding their own life.
Lorewells preserves the stories that help people understand where they fit: this is where you came from; this is what your people endured; this is what they loved; this is what they built; this is what they believed; this is what they learned; this is what they hope you remember.

3.4 Oral History as Historical Preservation
Traditional history often focuses on governments, institutions, wars, laws, economies, inventions, and famous individuals. This kind of history matters, but it is incomplete.

Official records can tell us what happened. Oral histories help us understand what it was like to live through what happened.

A military record can tell us where a veteran served. It cannot fully tell us what he feared, who he trusted, what he saw, how he changed, or how he carried those memories home. 
A newspaper can describe a fire. A firefighter can describe the heat, the smoke, the sounds, the decisions, and the faces he never forgot.
The Smithsonian Institution Archives describes oral history as a technique for generating and preserving original, historically interesting information from personal recollections through planned recorded interviews. 
The Oral History Association similarly defines oral history as a method of gathering, preserving, and interpreting the voices and memories of people, communities, and participants in past events.

Lorewells exists to preserve lived experience before it disappears from the record.
3.5 The Psychology of Life Review
In gerontology, life review is the process by which older adults reflect on their lives, revisit important memories, interpret experiences, and form a coherent understanding of what their life has meant.
This is not merely nostalgia. It can be an important psychological and emotional process. 
A meaningful life review allows a person to examine childhood, family, love, marriage, work, parenthood, failure, loss, regret, forgiveness, faith, service, accomplishment, mortality, and legacy.
Research on life review and reminiscence therapy has found associations with improvements in quality of life, life satisfaction, depression, anxiety, and psychospiritual well-being for older adults, depending on the population and design of the intervention.

Lorewells does not claim to provide therapy. But it does give people a structured opportunity to reflect deeply on their lives and preserve that reflection for others.
Many people reach old age and are never asked serious questions about their lives. 
Lorewells asks: What did your life teach you? 
What do you want your grandchildren to know? 
What did suffering teach you? 
What do you regret? 
What are you proud of? 
What do you believe matters most?
3.6 Storytelling, Trauma, and Meaning

Many of the people Lorewells may serve, especially veterans and first responders, carry difficult stories: war, violence, loss, emergency calls, accidents, death, moral injury, grief, brotherhood, and survival.

The goal of Lorewells is not therapy, and it should never claim to replace professional trauma care. But storytelling can be part of how human beings make meaning from difficult experiences.

A veteran may not want to describe every detail of combat, but he may want his grandchildren to understand what service cost him. 
A firefighter may not want to relive the worst call of his career, but he may want younger firefighters to understand courage, fear, and brotherhood.

Lorewells must approach these stories with reverence. The purpose is not to extract trauma. The purpose is to preserve truth. Participants should always remain in control of what they share.
3.7 The Social Value of Elder Wisdom

Modern culture often prioritizes youth, novelty, speed, and innovation. There is value in innovation, but when a culture only values what is new, it loses the wisdom of what is old.

Historically, elders served as repositories of cultural memory. They remembered droughts, wars, migrations, family patterns, moral failures, community changes, practical skills, and spiritual lessons.

Lorewells helps restore the dignity of elder wisdom. It says to an older person: 
You are not merely aging. 
You are carrying a library. 
You have seen things younger people have not seen. 
You have learned things younger people have not yet learned. 
You have stories that could shape a life after you are gone.
3.8 Veterans and First Responders

Lorewells has unique potential within veteran and first responder communities. These communities understand legacy, service, brotherhood, sacrifice, and the cost of duty.

Veterans and first responders often carry stories that are historically, emotionally, and morally significant: why they served, what fear felt like, how they handled responsibility, what brotherhood meant, how service affected family, what they wish civilians understood, and what they learned about courage and sacrifice.
For organizations serving these communities, Lorewells provides a way to honor members while they are still living. 
Not just with a plaque. 
Not just with a banquet. 
Not just with a photo on a wall. 
But with preserved testimony.

Lorewells can become a living wall of honor.


3.9 The Future Impact of Recorded Memory
High-quality long-form video preservation has implications that will grow over time. 
Today, a Lorewells interview may be watched by a family. In the future, it may be searchable by descendants, historians, students, churches, community organizations, or researchers.

Transcription, metadata, artificial intelligence, and digital archives will likely make these interviews more valuable over time. 
A future descendant may be able to search: 
What did my great-grandfather say about faith? 
What did my grandmother say about marriage? 
What did my ancestor say about Vietnam? 
What family stories did he tell about Oregon? 
What advice did she give to future generations?

Every day delayed means stories are permanently lost.

Every year, firsthand witnesses to major historical periods disappear.

Families often do not realize what they have lost until after the funeral. 
Then the questions come. 
Where exactly was Grandpa born? 
What did he experience in the war? 
How did Grandma meet him? 
What did she believe about God? 
Why did the family move? 
What did she regret?

Lorewells exists because the questions should be asked while the person is still here. The stories are still here. The wisdom is still here. The voices are still here. But not forever.

Every Person Is a Library. And the libraries are disappearing.


4. Funding & Partnership Proposal

Lorewells is especially focused on serving veterans, first responders, elders, community leaders, and individuals whose lives carry historical, cultural, family, or civic significance.

This proposal outlines a partnership model for organizations that want to preserve the stories of their members, honor their legacy, and create a permanent archive that will benefit families, communities, and future generations.

4.1 Mission Statement
Lorewells exists to professionally preserve the stories, memories, wisdom, and lived experiences of individuals so families, communities, and future generations can know where they came from, learn from those who came before them, and carry their legacy forward.

4.2 Vision Statement
A future where every family, organization, and community has access to the voices, stories, and wisdom of the people who shaped them.

4.3 Who Lorewells Serves
Veterans: Veterans carry firsthand memories of service, sacrifice, brotherhood, leadership, war, deployment, training, homecoming, and reintegration into civilian life. Lorewells gives veterans the opportunity to tell their story in a dignified, professional, and lasting way.
First Responders: Firefighters, law enforcement officers, EMTs, paramedics, dispatchers, search-and-rescue personnel, and corrections officers often experience decades of high-intensity service. Their stories include courage, sacrifice, trauma, humor, public service, leadership, and deep community knowledge.

Elders and Families: Many families want to preserve the stories of aging parents or grandparents but do not know how. Lorewells provides a professional, guided process that helps families preserve a loved one's life story with care, quality, and structure.

Historical Societies and Museums: Local history is often carried by ordinary people: ranchers, loggers, business owners, teachers, farmers, pastors, nurses, builders, veterans, and civic leaders.

Churches and Faith Communities: Faith communities often contain elders whose testimonies, spiritual journeys, sacrifices, and memories are deeply valuable.

Hospice, Senior Care, and End-of-Life Organizations: For individuals nearing the end of life, legacy work can be deeply meaningful. Lorewells can serve as a dignified legacy offering that allows people to leave behind a personal testimony for their families.

4.4 Why Organizations Should Fund This
Lorewells is fundable because it serves multiple meaningful outcomes at once. 
It is a legacy project, a historical preservation project, a family-strengthening project, a community memory project, a service-honoring project, an elder dignity project, a local history project, and a future-facing archive project.

For Veterans Organizations: Lorewells helps honor members while they are still living, preserve military stories for future generations, create educational resources, build a digital archive of member service, strengthen organizational identity, provide families with a lasting gift, and preserve stories before they are lost.

For First Responder Organizations: Lorewells helps honor years of service, preserve institutional memory, capture stories from retired personnel, create training and cultural resources, support peer connection, and preserve lessons learned from difficult calls, leadership, trauma, and public service.
For Community Foundations: Lorewells supports intergenerational connection, local history, elder dignity, civic memory, family resilience, cultural preservation, youth education, and community identity.

For Historical Societies: Lorewells helps move beyond artifacts alone. Objects, documents, and photographs matter. But a recorded human voice brings history alive.
For Families: Lorewells gives families something that may become more valuable than any 

material inheritance: the ability to hear a loved one speak after they are gone.

4.5 The Lorewells Interview Model
Each Lorewells interview is designed to be meaningful, respectful, and thorough. The process is not rushed. The goal is not to create a short promotional video. The goal is to preserve a life.

Typical interview length: 2 to 4 hours. Some interviews may be shorter. Some may require multiple sessions. The long-form format is essential because meaningful stories require time.

Production quality includes 4K cinema cameras, professional lenses, multiple camera angles, professional lighting, high-quality microphones, 32-bit float audio recording, stable tripod-based composition, careful audio monitoring, and documentary-style editing.

The tone is warm, respectful, patient, and human. The interviewer's role is not to dominate the conversation. The interviewer's role is to help the person remember.


4.6 Core Interview Categories

1. Origins: birthplace, childhood home, parents, grandparents, family values.
2. Childhood: earliest memories, school, friends, play, difficulty, beauty.
3. Family History: ancestry, traditions, migrations, hardships, inherited stories.
4. Work and Calling: first job, career, work ethic, lessons learned, what they built.
5. Service: military, public safety, ministry, community work, sacrifice, brotherhood, lessons.
6. Love and Marriage: meeting a spouse, commitment, family, parenting, love.
7. Hardship: difficult seasons, survival, help, suffering, resilience.
8. Faith and Meaning: belief, purpose, God, mortality, forgiveness, hope.
9. History: major events, wars, crises, cultural changes, national and local transformation.
10. Wisdom for Future Generations: mistakes to avoid, virtues that matter, what is worth fighting for, final advice.

4.7 Final Deliverables

Each funded Lorewells interview can include the full-length edited interview, clean audio mix, color correction, multi-camera edit, opening title card, subject name and date, chapter markers, transcript, optional short highlight film, private family viewing link, digital download file, long-term archive copy, organization-facing archive listing, optional social media clip package, and optional physical USB drive or hard drive delivery.

4.8 Ownership and Access Models
Private Family Archive: The interview is created for the family only. The family receives a private link and/or downloadable file. The video is not publicly listed.

Organization Archive: The interview is created in partnership with an organization. The family gives permission for the interview to appear on the organization's website or archive page.

Public Historical Archive: The interview is preserved as part of a public oral history collection. The subject signs a release allowing public access.

Hybrid Model: Some portions are public while other portions remain private. For example, a veteran's full three-hour interview may be private to family and organization members, while a 5-minute highlight video is public.

4.14 Ethical Considerations

Consent: Participants must clearly understand how their interview will be used. They should be able to choose whether the interview is private, shared with family, shared with an organization, or made public.

Sensitive Topics: Participants should never be pressured to discuss traumatic or private experiences. The interview should invite honesty but honor boundaries.
Family Access: Families should have clear access to the final interview. If public release is allowed, the family should know what that means.

Historical Accuracy: Lorewells preserves personal memory. Memory is valuable, but it is not always the same as verified history. Interviews should be framed as firsthand accounts, personal testimony, and lived experience.

Dignity: The editing should never exploit, sensationalize, or manipulate the subject. The person's life should be handled with reverence.

5. The Lorewells Apprenticeship

Lorewells has a secondary mission: to train the next generation of media storytellers through hands-on apprenticeship.
Every Lorewells interview requires real production skill. 
A life story must be filmed with care. 
Audio must be captured clearly. 
Cameras must be operated with discipline. 
Lighting must honor the subject. 
Files must be organized. 
Footage must be edited. 
Audio must be mixed. 
Color must be corrected. 
Transcripts must be created. 
Archives must be prepared. 
Deliverables must be uploaded, stored, and presented with professionalism.

These are not abstract skills. They are the skills of a modern media craftsman.

Lorewells creates an opportunity for young men and women to learn these skills by participating in meaningful work. Rather than learning only in a classroom, interns and apprentices can learn through real production experience, guided by experienced media professionals.

They can learn how to prepare for an interview; set up cameras; frame a subject; light a room; capture clean professional audio; monitor levels; organize media files; back up footage safely; edit long-form interviews; mix and master audio; color correct footage; create transcripts and chapter markers; export for web delivery; upload and archive final files; handle copyrighted material properly; communicate with families and organizations; tell stories with dignity; and serve the person in front of the camera.

This is more than technical training. It is formation. A young person learning media through Lorewells is not merely learning how to make content. They are learning how to preserve memory, how to listen, how to honor elders, how to handle sensitive stories, and how to treat human beings with dignity.

Media can be service. Media can be preservation. Media can be legacy work. Media can be an act of love.
In this way, Lorewells becomes a bridge between generations. The older generation shares wisdom. The younger generation learns the craft required to preserve it. One generation gives the story. Another generation learns how to carry it forward.
Lorewells preserves the stories of one generation and trains the storytellers of the next.

5.1 Apprenticeship Model

Pre-Production: Apprentices learn participant communication, scheduling, question preparation, release forms, location planning, gear packing, shot planning, and production checklists.

Production: Apprentices assist on real interview shoots. They learn camera placement, lens choice, lighting setup, audio setup, lavalier or boom placement, room tone, data management, and respectful set behavior.

Post-Production: Apprentices learn file organization, syncing cameras, audio cleanup, long-form editing, pacing, color correction, audio mixing, transcript creation, chapter markers, exports, revisions, and final delivery.

Archival Delivery: Apprentices learn file naming, backups, cloud delivery, archive structure, metadata, permissions, copyright awareness, family access, and long-term preservation.

Story Ethics: Apprentices learn consent, dignity, privacy, trauma awareness, historical humility, family sensitivity, and the difference between telling a story and exploiting one.

5.2 Why Funders Should Care
The apprenticeship component gives Lorewells an additional public benefit. A funded Lorewells pilot does not only preserve the stories of veterans, first responders, elders, and community members. It also helps train young people in valuable media skills.

Funding supports historical preservation, veteran and first responder legacy, elder dignity, family strengthening, workforce development, creative training, youth mentorship, intergenerational connection, and practical career skills.

A funder can say: We helped preserve the stories of our elders while training young people to become skilled, ethical media storytellers.

The Smithsonian Institution Archives describes oral history as a technique for generating and preserving original, historically interesting information from personal recollections through planned recorded interviews.

The Oral History Association defines oral history as a field and method for gathering, preserving, and interpreting the voices and memories of people, communities, and participants in past events.

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses of reminiscence therapy and life review interventions report benefits for older adults in areas such as quality of life, life satisfaction, depression, anxiety, and psychospiritual well-being, though outcomes vary by population and intervention design.

National Endowment for the Humanities materials describe oral histories as a way for communities to preserve cultural memory in their own voices and contribute under-recorded perspectives to the broader humanities record.
